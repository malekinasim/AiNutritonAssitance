# Drinking water
Water is the best drink for quenching thirst. How much a person needs varies with body size, physical activity and temperature. People need more fluid during hot weather, exercise or illness with fever. A simple sign of good hydration is pale yellow urine.

# Vegetables and fruit
Eating plenty of vegetables, fruit and berries lowers the risk of heart disease and some cancers. Many Nordic dietary guidelines suggest about 500 grams per day, roughly five handfuls. Variety matters: different colours provide different vitamins and minerals.

# Dietary fiber
Fiber supports digestion, helps keep blood sugar stable and makes meals more filling. Adults are generally advised to eat about 25 to 35 grams of fiber per day. Good sources are whole grains, beans, lentils, vegetables, fruit, nuts and seeds. Increase fiber slowly and drink enough water to avoid stomach discomfort.

# Whole grains
Choosing whole grain bread, pasta, rice and oats instead of refined white versions gives more fiber, vitamins and minerals. Whole grains are linked to a lower risk of type 2 diabetes and heart disease. Look for products where whole grain is the first ingredient.

# Protein needs
Healthy adults generally need about 0.8 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day. People who do regular strength training may benefit from around 1.2 to 2.0 grams per kilogram. Good sources include fish, chicken, eggs, dairy, beans, lentils, tofu and nuts. Spreading protein across meals helps the body use it well.

# Fish and seafood
Eating fish two to three times a week is commonly recommended, with at least one meal of fatty fish such as salmon, herring or mackerel. Fatty fish provides omega-3 fats and vitamin D, which support heart health.

# Fats
The type of fat matters more than the total amount. Replacing saturated fat (butter, fatty meat, cream) with unsaturated fat (vegetable oils, nuts, seeds, avocado, fish) lowers the risk of heart disease. Rapeseed oil and olive oil are good choices for cooking.

# Added sugar
The World Health Organization recommends that added sugars make up less than 10 percent of daily energy intake, and ideally less than 5 percent. The largest sources are often soft drinks, sweets, pastries and sweetened yogurts. Sugar naturally present in whole fruit is not counted as added sugar.

# Salt
The World Health Organization recommends eating less than 5 grams of salt per day, about one teaspoon. Most salt comes from processed foods such as bread, cheese, processed meat and ready meals, not from the salt shaker. Too much salt raises blood pressure.

# Red and processed meat
Eating a lot of red meat and processed meat is linked to a higher risk of colorectal cancer. Many guidelines advise limiting red meat to about 350 grams of cooked meat per week and eating as little processed meat (sausages, bacon, ham) as possible.

# Healthy weight loss
A moderate energy deficit leads to steady weight loss. Losing about 0.5 to 1 kilogram per week is generally considered safe for most adults. Very low calorie diets and fast weight loss can cause muscle loss and nutrient deficiencies and should only be done with medical supervision. Combining a balanced diet with physical activity works best long term.

# Regular meals
Eating at regular times, such as breakfast, lunch, dinner and one or two snacks, helps keep energy and blood sugar stable and makes it easier to avoid overeating later in the day. Meals that combine protein, fiber and vegetables keep you full longer.

# Physical activity
The World Health Organization recommends that adults do 150 to 300 minutes of moderate activity, or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous activity, per week. Muscle-strengthening activities should be done at least two days a week. Even short walks are better than sitting still for long periods.

# Sleep
Most adults need 7 to 9 hours of sleep per night. Poor sleep is linked to increased appetite, weight gain and lower concentration. A regular sleep schedule and avoiding large meals, caffeine and screens before bed can improve sleep quality.

# Vitamin D
In Nordic countries the sun is too weak during the winter months for the skin to produce enough vitamin D. Food sources include fatty fish, eggs and vitamin D fortified milk and spreads. Some groups may need a supplement; this is best discussed with a healthcare professional.

# Alcohol
Alcohol provides a lot of energy but no nutrients, and it increases the risk of several diseases including cancer. From a health perspective, the less alcohol the better. Alcohol can also interact with many medications.
